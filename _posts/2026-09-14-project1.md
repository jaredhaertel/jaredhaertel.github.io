## Background on the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Sergey Mikhaylovich Prokudin-Gorskii was a Russian photographer and chemist whom documented the Russian Empire photographically between 1909 and 1915 for the purposes of educating schoolchildren via colored projections. Using a darkroom railroad-car provided by Tsar Nicholas II, Prokudin-Groskii recorded colored scenes by taking three different exposures using a red, green, and blue filter. The Library of Congress purchased some of these negatives in 1948 which have since been digitized in high-resolution and made available online. The goal of this assignment is to align the different segments to form a colorized image.

## Alignment
We begin by roughly dividing the raw images into three segments, corresponding to the blue channel at the top, the green channel in the middle, and the red channel at the bottom. Due to slight amount of variations in the margins and how it was scanned, putting these three images on top of each-other would create a misaligned image!

<figure style="width: 50%; margin: 0 auto 1.5em auto; margin-top: 2em;">
    <img src="/assets/images/project1/web_images/selfie_stacked_bad.jpg" alt="Naive unaligned color channel composite" style="width: 100%; height: auto; display: block; margin: 0 auto 0.5em;">
    <figcaption>Figure 2: Naive channel stacking without alignment</figcaption>
</figure>

To resolve this, we will somehow align the blue and red channels to the green channel. In this way, we've simplified the problem two that of aligning one grayscale image to another of equal dimension. We can imagine each channel as being a surface, with corresponding objects having a similar pattern, up to, say, its brightness in that channel. For instance a patch of grass will be high frequency irrespective of channel, while an apple might be more smooth in each of its color channels.

With this intuition, over a search window $\mathcal{W}$, we wish to find the translation $(x, y) \in \mathcal{W}$ of the image $i_1$ minimizing some heuristic $d(i_1, i_2)$, where $i_1$ and $i_2$ are two images of equal dimension. Two candidates for this heuristic we explore is the $L_2$-norm, defined by 

$$
\lVert i \rVert_{L_2} = \sqrt{\sum_{x=1}^W \sum_{y=1}^H i_{x, y}^2}
,$$

which induces the heuristic $\lVert i_1 - i_2 \rVert\_{L_2}$, and the Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC), defined as 

$$
d(i_1, i_2) = \sum_{x=1}^W \sum_{y=1}^H (\hat{i}_1)_{x,y} (\hat{i}_2)_{x,y}
$$

where $\hat{i} = \frac{i - \mathrm{mean}(i)}{\mathrm{std}(i)}$ is the normalized image. Note in the case of NCC, we wish to maximize the heuristic, which is conceptually the same as a minimization problem by negation. A micro-optimization we do in the case of NCC is normalizing before cropping so that we don't need to normalize in each loop, which is technically incorrect as we can no longer guarantee the cross-correlation is between -1 and 1. In practice, however, I found that this works just as good as we are simply finding the maximum NCC.

As we want to align the contents of the image and not the borders, we cut off 15% from each side and align this instead. For the sake of memory efficiency, we have a loop over $\mathcal{W}$ and take the corresponding numpy view of each image, where we then apply the heuristic to find the best candidate. The numpy view is crucial here, as when we crop and shift we aren't actually creating a new image in memory, but merely taking a different view of the same data. Below I show the results for the $L_2$ heuristic on JPEG images, which are roughly the size of $(W, H) = (390, 340)$. Here, the search window is a square centered at zero (the identity shift) with a width of 21.

### Side note: Why align with the green channel?

The starter code for this project had the red and green channels align with the blue channel, but this didn't work out well for the Emir of Bukhara's deep blue silk robe. This is because the blue robe has very strong intensity in the blue channel but weak intensity in the red channel, as the robe absorbed red light. This gap in intensity violates the structural assumption of the $L_2$ metric: each channel's surface looks the same. One would think that the normalization used for NCC would help, but it doesn't as the correlation is also global. For instance, the clothes intensity in the red channel are lower than average, while in the blue channel the intensity of the clothes are above average, and so the product in that region is negative. However, the green channel works as a good compromise between the two channels. Perhaps the reason for this is because green wavelengths are in the middle of the spectrum, and so it is possible the green filter bled some blue and red light through. Dually, the silk robe reflects some green light decreasing the contrast gap between blue-green and green-red.

## Aligning high resolution images via image pyramids

For our high resolution images, we have a problem! The issue is that the size of the search window needed is proportional to the resolution of the image. To get around this, we use an image pyramid, a dyadic series of downscaled images, with each subsequent image whose height and width is halved. The idea is to start at the lowest resolution with a larger search window, a square of length 21 centered at $[0,0]$, where computing the heuristics is cheap. One then refines the estimates by doubling the previous translation when descending the image pyramid to match the scale of the current level, followed by a refinement search over a smaller window of size, a square of length 7 centered at the scaled previous guess. The size of these windows were found by trial and error. We use an image pyramid of size 4 (including the original image) where the initial image is around the size $(W, H) = (3700, 3200)$ and the final image is around the size $(460, 400)$. All-in-all, the whole process takes around only one second per image on my M4 Macbook Air.

### Dangers of downscaling

One must be careful when downscaling images, as simply taking every other column and row will produce weird artifacts called aliasing. This is caused by the lower resolution undersampling, meaning higher frequency image data can't be resolved. To solve this, scikit-image's rescale function uses a Gaussian blur before downscaling to prevent aliasing.

### Alignment discussion
The closest thing to misalignment appear in the Melons, Harvesters, and Three Generations images. The thing they all have in common is a well-aligned background but the subjects are not completely aligned. I believe this is because some individuals moved slightly between the different color exposures, which is particularly noticeable in the Harvesters image.
