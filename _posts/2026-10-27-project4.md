In Part A, we compute homographies from hand-picked point correspondences and use them to rectify images and stitch photos into mosaics. In Part B, which will be added at a later date, we automate this correspondence step.

# Part A: Image Warping and Mosaicing

## Part A.1: Shooting the Pictures

For each mosaic I fixed the center of projection and rotated the camera between shots, so consecutive images are related by a projective transformation. All photos were downscaled by a factor of 4 before computing homographies and warping since my Pixel 9 outputs very high resolution images, which causes some memory troubles. Alternatively, I could process images in chunks at the cost of code complexity.

I tried to get ~40% overlap between images. Focus and exposure for each shot were automatic. The source images for each mosaic are shown with their correspondences in Part A.2 and Part A.4.

## Part A.2: Recovering Homographies

A homography $$H$$ maps a point $$(x, y)$$ in one image to $$(x', y')$$ in another via

$$
\begin{bmatrix} wx' \\ wy' \\ w \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Fixing $$h_{33} = 1$$ leaves 8 unknowns. Expanding $$x' = \frac{h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + 1}$$ (and similarly for $$y'$$) and clearing the denominator, each correspondence gives two linear equations:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \\
0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y'
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ h_{13} \\ h_{21} \\ h_{22} \\ h_{23} \\ h_{31} \\ h_{32} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Stacking these for $$n \ge 4$$ correspondences gives a $$2n \times 8$$ system $$A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{b}$$, which `computeH` solves with least squares (`np.linalg.lstsq`). Using more than 4 points makes the solution less sensitive to clicking error, which is very necessary since clicking accurately is surprisingly hard.

In the above homography matrix, we see some minor skewing as noted by the off-diagonal entries in the top-left 2×2 submatrix. The right column indicates translation, which we see a lot of. The bottom row seems to play a very weak role due to its low values, meaning the transformation is dominated by scaling, translation, and some skewing.

In general, correspondences were selected using [this tool](https://cal-cs180.github.io/fa23/hw/proj3/tool.html) made by another student from a past year. For rectification, I used [PixSpy](https://pixspy.com) to see pixel coordinate locations. I primarily selected sharp corners spread throughout the image. Things like windows or corners of some rectangular frame are generally much easier to select accurately. Having correspondences over a wider area helps keep the images aligned where they overlap since if we only selected correspondences in a small area, then errors would propagate towards the outer regions where they overlap.

## Part A.3: Warping the Images

Both warps use inverse warping. I first push the four corners of the input through $$H$$ to find the bounding box of the output, then map every output pixel back through $$H^{-1}$$ to find where to sample in the input. Each warp returns an RGBA image whose alpha channel marks which output pixels landed inside the source image.

- **Nearest neighbor** (`warpImageNearestNeighbor`) rounds each inverse-mapped coordinate to the closest pixel.
- **Bilinear** (`warpImageBilinear`) takes a weighted average of the four surrounding pixels, with weights given by the fractional offsets $$d_x, d_y$$.

### Rectification

To test the warps, I rectify planar objects photographed at an angle. I click the four corners of a rectangle of known aspect ratio (measured with my handy-dandy ruler) and compute the homography that sends them to an axis-aligned rectangle. For each example I show the full warp and a version cropped to just the rectified rectangle, where the differences between the two interpolation methods are easier to see.

### Nearest Neighbor vs. Bilinear

The differences are more subtle on the landscape painting, with nearest neighbor looking a bit more jagged and bilinear interpolation looking smoother (since we are taking a weighted average over pixels in the inverse projection). The difference is far more apparent in the lettering, with bilinear interpolation looking much smoother. Rectifying onto a smaller footprint makes the differences more pronounced. Nearest neighbor is strictly faster as we just need to round the inverse coordinates to the nearest center while for bilinear interpolation we must do a weighted average across four points, but in reality the compute is quite negligible as most of the cost is in computing the inverse grid and the projection matrix.

## Part A.4: Blending Images into a Mosaic

### Procedure

`make_mosaic` takes a set of images, a set of pairwise correspondences, and a base image whose plane everything is projected onto.

1. Compute a homography for each correspondence pair and build an undirected graph over the images, storing $$H$$ on one edge direction and $$H^{-1}$$ on the other.
2. Run a breadth-first search from the base image, composing homographies along the way so every image gets a homography into the base image's frame. This means images don't need to be directly matched with the base image.
3. Push all image corners through their homographies to get the bounds of the final mosaic.
4. Bilinearly warp each image into that shared canvas. The first image is placed directly, and each later image is blended into the running mosaic.

Strictly speaking, this graph-based approach is overkill for the mosaics below, since I only use at most 3 images. The hope is that in Part B I can take several photographs, and once pairwise correspondences are computed automatically, this code will still work.

### Blending

I tried three blending methods, all of which were explained in lecture, with the results shown below on the night mosaic.

- **Weighted average:** overlapping pixels are averaged together. This is done by adding up all the warped images and dividing by the alpha channel, as this counts the number of images that contributed to that pixel.
- **Distance transform:** each pixel's weight is its distance to the nearest edge of that image's valid region (`distance_transform_edt` on the alpha mask), and the two images are averaged with weights $$d_1 / (d_1 + d_2)$$ and $$d_2 / (d_1 + d_2)$$. This fades each image out toward its border.
- **Two-band:** each image is split into a low-frequency band (an alpha-aware Gaussian blur, which divides by the blurred alpha so the black outside the image doesn't bleed in) and a high-frequency band (the residual). The low band is combined with the distance-weighted average above, while the high band is taken entirely from whichever image has the larger distance value at that pixel. This keeps sharp detail from being ghosted while still smoothing out exposure differences.
